# How to Wash Dishes

## Dishwashing in the 21st Century

Washing dishes looks like the simplest job in the kitchen. As modern kitchen tools become more and more complex, "washing dishes" has long gone beyond its literal meaning, yet many people's idea of dishwashing is still stuck at the "ancient method" of "wiping dishes with a towel". Some households have even built up health hazards (such as carcinogens) through long-term unscientific dishwashing habits. To help everyone master a scientific, efficient and modern way of washing dishes, this guide covers the process, the precautions, and the common pitfalls in detail.

## The Goal of Washing Dishes

Whether you wash by hand or use a dishwasher, there is only one goal: return the tableware to a state where it can **safely be used again**. Specifically, all of the following must be met:

1. **Looks clean**: no grease film, water spots, food bits or fingerprints on the surface.
2. **Feels clean**: run a finger across the bottom of the bowl, the handle of the spoon or the inside of the cup rim. If it doesn't feel sticky, it's clean.
3. **Smells clean**: no rancid oil smell and no harsh detergent smell.
4. **Dry and ready to use**: no obvious standing water after draining or drying, and not damp when put away.
5. **No cross-contamination**: tools used for raw meat have not contaminated the clean tableware.

Only when all 5 are met is it truly clean.

## Tools to Prepare

Modern kitchenware comes in many materials (non-stick coatings, cast iron, enamel, wood, stainless steel...), and different materials need different cleaning tools. Choosing the wrong tool is itself a common mistake. Washing supplies are already assumed in the kitchen preparation guide; when washing dishes, make sure the following are available:

```text
Hot water (preferred), cold water, sink, washing basin (optional), dish sponge / scrub pad
Dish soap, steel wool (stainless steel cookware only; never on non-stick/coated surfaces)
Drying rack, clean dishcloth or paper towels, gloves (optional)
Dishwasher (optional, see below)
```

### Key Points for Using the Tools

- **Sponge / scrub pad**: the first choice for everyday bowls and plates. After use, squeeze it dry and let it air out so the sponge itself doesn't get moldy and smelly.
- **Steel wool**: only for hard, uncoated surfaces such as iron woks and stainless steel pots. **Never** use it on non-stick pans, ceramic coatings or fine porcelain, or you will destroy the coating.
- **Dishcloth**: the cloth for drying dishes must be kept separate from the cloths for wiping the stove and the floor. Mixing them means all your washing was for nothing.
- **Dishwasher**: if you have one, you can skip the main hand-washing process, but you still need to scrape off food beforehand and put things away afterward. See the "Dishwasher" section below.

## Overall Process

Many people's dishwashing has only two steps, "add dish soap → rinse", which is far from enough. A scientific dishwashing process should include the following stages:

```text
Collect and sort → Scrape and soak → Wash → Rinse off detergent → Inspect → Dry → Put away
```

Each step has an irreplaceable role, and skipping any of them may leave a hazard behind. Each step is explained below.

## Detailed Steps

### 1. Collect and Sort

After the meal, first gather the bowls, plates, chopsticks, spoons, pots, cutting boards and so on from wherever they are, bring them to the sink, and arrange them by how dirty they are.

Wash them in this order (from lightest to heaviest):

1. **Lightly soiled**: glasses, cups, chopsticks, spoons
2. **Moderately soiled**: serving plates, rice bowls
3. **Heavily soiled**: woks, deep fryers, baking trays, containers with egg on them
4. **High risk**: cutting boards and knives used for raw meat or raw fish (wash these separately at the end)

Why sort: if you wash the lightly soiled items first, the water and sponge are still fairly clean and won't carry heavy grease onto the cups. Tools used for raw food are washed separately at the end, so pathogens aren't carried onto clean tableware.

### 2. Scrape and Soak

Many people skip this step, but it directly determines how hard the washing will be. Dealing with stubborn residue in advance means you don't have to scrub over and over when you actually wash, saving both effort and water.

How to do it:

1. Scrape off large pieces of food with a napkin or spatula and put them in the food waste bin. **Do not wash them straight down the drain**; it clogs easily.
2. Soak in these cases:
   - Dried-on rice grains or starch paste
   - Set protein residue (egg, milk residue)
   - Burnt or scorched pot bottoms
3. How to soak:
   - **Ordinary grease, rice grains**: soak in hot water for 5–15 minutes
   - **Scorched pot bottom**: soak in hot water + a little dish soap, or first add water, bring it to a boil, then turn off the heat and let it sit covered for 10–20 minutes
   - **Tea stains or limescale on stainless steel**: soak with a little white vinegar, then scrub (don't mix with bleach products)

> [!Warning]
> **Don't run cold water into a pan of oil that hasn't cooled down.** Thermal expansion and contraction can warp the pan, and the oil may spatter and burn you when it hits cold water.
> **Don't soak cast iron or carbon steel pans for long**, or they will rust easily. Dry them immediately after washing and coat them with a thin layer of oil.

### 3. Wash: Remove Grease and Grime

This is the core step of the whole process. Warm to hot water (about 40–50°C) is recommended. Hot water effectively lowers the viscosity of grease and greatly improves cleaning efficiency. If you have a temperature-adjustable faucet, set it to the right temperature.

Standard hand-washing steps:

1. Turn on the faucet, or fill the washing basin with warm water.
2. Squeeze a little dish soap onto the sponge (the size of a mung bean to a soybean, about 1–2ml is enough) and work up a lather.
3. Scrub in the order "outside → inside, handle → head, edge → center":
   - **Bowls**: scrub the outside first, then the inside, focusing on the inside bottom
   - **Plates**: the surface, the rim, the bottom
   - **Chopsticks and spoons**: the tips of the chopsticks and the bowl of the spoon are the main contamination areas
   - **Pots and pans**: the bottom, the sides, the rim, and don't forget grease splatters on the outside
4. For stubborn stains, first scrub that spot hard with the rough side of the sponge. Don't reach for steel wool right away.
5. Cutting boards and knives:
   - Raw meat cutting board: after scrubbing with dish soap, scald it with hot water. Disinfect regularly when you can.
   - Knives: scrub with the blade facing away from you to avoid cuts.

More dish soap doesn't mean cleaner. Using too much just makes rinsing more work, and leaving residue on the dishes is not good either.

### 4. Rinse: Remove the Detergent

After washing, rinse off the detergent and dissolved grime thoroughly with clean water.

How to do it:

1. Rinse each item thoroughly under running water until all of the following are true:
   - No foam
   - No slippery feeling when you touch it
   - The water film on the surface is even (if grease hasn't been fully removed, water beads up instead of spreading evenly)
2. Cup rims, grooves on bowl bottoms and handle joints tend to hold detergent, so rinse them a few seconds longer.
3. If rinsing in a still basin, change the water at least once, then give everything a final pass under running water.

### 5. Inspect

Go over each item:

- **Look**: hold it up to the light and check for a reflective grease film
- **Touch**: lightly touch the inside with your fingertip and see if it's sticky
- **Smell**: sniff it up close for any sour or detergent smell

Anything that fails goes back to step 3 for spot washing. Don't put something away to dry with dirty spots still on it.

### 6. Dry

Clean doesn't mean ready to use—a damp environment is a breeding ground for bacteria. Many people think the job is done once the dishes are washed, but if you put them away without drying, all the earlier effort may be wasted.

Options:

| Method | How | When to use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Drain | Place upside down on a drying rack and let air dry | Most common day to day, least effort |
| Wipe dry | Wipe with a clean, dedicated cloth until no obvious water drops remain | When you need to put things away right away; prevents water spots on glasses |
| Hot air dry | Dishwasher drying mode or warm air | Large batches |

Notes:

- When draining, place items **upside down or on their side** as much as possible so water doesn't pool in the hollow of bowls and plates.
- If the cloth itself already smells or is damp and sticky, wash the cloth first before using it on the dishes.
- Don't dry dishes with a cloth you just used to wipe the stove.

### 7. Put Away

Once you've confirmed the dishes are dry, put them back in the cupboard or dish rack.

A few basic rules:

1. **Don't put away until dry**: damp dishes in a cupboard breed bacteria and odors.
2. Before stacking bowls and plates, make sure the bottoms are clean, so you don't press a dirty bottom onto the inside of the bowl beneath it.
3. Place chopsticks, spoons and forks head up, or in the direction your drying basket is designed for, and keep them ventilated.
4. Store knives and cutting boards used for raw food separately from everyday tableware.

## Recommended Washing Order

If you are washing a full set at once, use this order:

```text
1. Glasses / cups
2. Chopsticks, spoons, forks
3. Plates, bowls
4. Spatulas, ladles and other cooking utensils
5. Woks / pots (heavy grease)
6. Raw food cutting boards and knives (last, and wash the sink separately afterward)
7. Rinse the sink and faucet handle (finishing up)
```

The logic is: go from least to most contaminated, so heavy grease later doesn't dirty the items you have already washed.

## Handling Different Materials

Modern kitchens routinely mix cookware and tableware of many different materials, but the right way to clean them varies a lot. The wrong method can damage the item at best, and create a health hazard at worst.

### Non-stick Pans

- Use the soft side of a sponge + neutral dish soap and scrub gently.
- **Never use steel wool or hard scrapers on the coating**, or the coating will be destroyed and the pan ruined.
- Dry after washing; don't leave water sitting in it for long.

### Cast Iron / Carbon Steel Pans

- Use little or no dish soap, to protect the oil film (seasoning) on the surface.
- Hot water + a brush is enough to remove residue.
- After washing, wipe dry, dry over low heat, and coat with a thin layer of cooking oil to prevent rust.

### Wooden Cutting Boards / Wooden Spatulas

- Wash quickly; don't soak for long, or the wood will crack and warp.
- Stand upright to dry after washing.
- Rub with cooking oil from time to time for maintenance (cutting boards).

### Chopsticks

- Focus on the end that touches food (the tips).
- Bamboo and wooden chopsticks easily hold water and crack, so always let them dry after washing.
- If they become seriously moldy or cracked, replace them; don't keep using them.

### Dishwasher

A dishwasher is one of the biggest efficiency boosters in a modern kitchen, but it is not a magic machine where you "just throw the dishes in". It still needs your help to work its best:

1. Scrape off large food scraps (bones, leaves, etc.) first, or they will clog the filter and spray holes.
2. Load the dishes according to the dishwasher manual: bowls face down, plates on their side, and don't block the rotation of the spray arms.
3. Choose the right program—heavy duty or high temperature for heavy grease, quick or eco for light everyday soiling.
4. After washing, open it and check, especially the grooves on bowl bottoms and the inside of cup rims. Hand wash anything that isn't clean.
5. Clean the dishwasher filter and door seal regularly to prevent odors and mold. Clean the filter every 1–2 weeks and run an empty cycle with dishwasher cleaner once a month.

### Air Fryer

The basket and the drip tray are the main things to clean in an air fryer. Many people just give them a wipe and put them back; over time the grease carbonizes, which affects heating and food safety.

- After each use, wait for the machine to cool, then remove the basket and drip tray and wash them with warm water + dish soap + a sponge.
- The mesh of the basket easily traps food bits; an old toothbrush or small brush helps clean it.
- **Don't scrub the basket with steel wool**—most baskets have a non-stick coating, and steel wool will damage it, leading to sticking and peeling later.
- If grease has splattered inside the body of the machine, wipe it with a damp cloth. **Never immerse the body of the machine in water**.
- If the heating element (at the top) has grease on it, wipe it gently with a damp cloth after it cools. Grease left there for a long time will smoke at high temperatures and may even produce harmful substances.

### Rice Cooker

Most rice cooker inner pots have a non-stick coating, so be careful to protect it when washing. Many people overlook cleaning the steam vent and the inside of the lid.

- Wash the inner pot with the soft side of a sponge + dish soap. **No steel wool**. Once the coating is scratched and peeling, rice will stick, and flakes of coating will end up in your food.
- The inside of the lid, the steam vent and the seal are easily overlooked—starchy steam condenses there into a sticky residue that turns sour and smelly over time. Wipe the inside of the lid after every use, and remove and wash the seal regularly.
- Only wipe the outer body with a damp cloth. **Do not immerse it in water**, or you may damage the electronics.

### Pressure Cooker / Electric Pressure Cooker

The key to cleaning a pressure cooker is not the pot itself, but the safety-related parts: the sealing ring, the pressure release valve and the lid. If these aren't cleaned properly, it is not only unhygienic but may also affect the seal, which is a safety hazard.

- **Sealing ring**: remove it after each use and wash it with warm water + dish soap. Food bits and grease hide in the groove of the ring, so scrub carefully. Let it dry before putting it back. Replace the ring promptly when it ages (hardens, deforms or cracks).
- **Release valve / pressure limiting valve**: remove and rinse with clean water, making sure the valve hole is clear. If food bits block the valve hole, pressure may not release properly next time.
- **Lid**: grease and food build up on the inside and in the groove around the edge, so scrub it clean.
- **Inner pot**: clean it the same way as an ordinary stainless steel or non-stick pot, choosing tools based on the material.

### Oven

The oven's baking tray, rack and interior walls all need regular cleaning. Many people only wash the tray and ignore the rack and walls; over time the grease carbonizes and gives off a smoky smell that can even affect the flavor of food.

- **Baking tray**: wash it while still warm (careful not to burn yourself) with dish soap + a sponge. Once cooled, the grease solidifies and is harder to remove. For stubborn baked-on residue, soak in hot water for 15–30 minutes before scrubbing. Lining the tray with foil or parchment paper makes cleaning much easier.
- **Rack**: the gaps in a wire rack are hard to clean. Soak it in the sink in hot water + dish soap, then scrub with a brush.
- **Interior walls**: after cooling, wipe with a damp cloth + a little dish soap. If grease has already carbonized and turned black, make a paste of baking soda + water, spread it on the stains, leave it for 1–2 hours, then wipe. Don't scrape the oven walls with steel wool; it can damage the surface coating.
- Some ovens have a self-cleaning function (high-temperature pyrolysis). Use it according to the manual, but you still need to wipe away the ash afterward.

### Microwave

The inside walls and turntable of a microwave easily get dirty from splattered sauces and grease. Many people never clean them; over time it smells, and old food bits get reheated again and again, giving a burnt smell.

- **Turntable**: remove it and wash it like ordinary glass/porcelain.
- **Interior walls**: wipe with a damp cloth + a little dish soap. If there is dried-on food splatter, put a bowl of water with a few slices of lemon in the microwave and heat on medium for 3–5 minutes. The steam softens the grime and makes wiping much easier.
- **Door seal**: the seal around the door frame is an easily overlooked corner where grease and crumbs build up in the gaps. Wipe it regularly with a damp cloth.
- **Don't use cleaning tools containing metal** on the inside of the microwave, to avoid damaging the interior coating.

### Steamer / Steamer Trays

The most common problems after using a steamer are limescale and starch residue.

- **Stainless steel steamer trays**: wash normally with dish soap + a sponge. The holes easily trap bits of dough (from the bottoms of mantou and baozi); clean them promptly, or they'll dry and become very hard to remove.
- **Bamboo steamer baskets**: don't use dish soap; just scrub with hot water + a brush. Bamboo absorbs the smell of detergent, which will seep into food the next time you steam. Always let it air dry after washing, or it will easily get moldy. Throw away moldy bamboo steamers.
- **Steamer pot body**: limescale (white crystals) easily builds up on the bottom. Soak in white vinegar for 10–20 minutes, then scrub.

### Spatulas / Ladles / Slotted Spoons

You use these every day, but the cleaning method depends on the material:

- **Stainless steel**: the toughest; you can scrub it with steel wool without worrying about scratches. Pay attention to the gap where the handle joins, where grease likes to hide.
- **Silicone**: just wash with a sponge + dish soap. Silicone is heat resistant but easily absorbs colors and smells (especially curry and ketchup); if badly stained, soak in baking soda water. Don't scrub silicone with steel wool; it leaves scratches that hold dirt more easily.
- **Wood**: like wooden spatulas and cutting boards, wash quickly, dry quickly, no soaking. See the "Wooden Cutting Boards / Wooden Spatulas" section above.

### Peeler

The gap between the blade and the slot of a peeler is very small, and vegetable peel fibers and starch easily get stuck in it. Many people just rinse it under the tap after use, which doesn't actually get it clean.

- Use an old toothbrush to scrub the gap along the direction of the blade (mind the direction to avoid cuts).
- Shake off the water after washing and let it dry in a ventilated spot. Even a stainless steel peeler will rust and dull if it stays damp for long periods.

### Kettle / Electric Kettle

The main problem with a kettle isn't grease, it's limescale.

- **Descaling**: add white vinegar or citric acid (about 1–2 tablespoons), fill with water, bring to a boil, turn off and let it soak for 30–60 minutes. Pour it out and rinse 2–3 times with clean water. In areas with hard water, descale every 1–2 weeks.
- Don't scrub the inside of the kettle with steel wool; it scratches the stainless steel surface, and scratches make limescale stick more easily.
- Limescale also builds up in the spout and on the inside of the lid, so clean those too.

## Common Problems and Pitfalls

Below are the most common mistakes and misconceptions when washing dishes, organized by type. Each comes with its concrete consequences—only by knowing "why not" can you really avoid these pitfalls.

### Washing Habits

- **Rinsing without scrubbing**: water alone can't remove a grease film. Grease doesn't dissolve in water; it has to be emulsified by the surfactants in dish soap and then removed by the friction of the sponge. If you only rinse, the bowl looks clean but still has a thin layer of grease that feels slippery, and the next time you serve food in it, the food picks up the smell of rancid oil.
- **Using too much dish soap**: more foam doesn't mean cleaner. Beyond a certain concentration, detergent doesn't clean any better. Using too much just makes it hard to rinse off, and the leftover detergent gets eaten with your next meal. Long-term intake of detergent residue won't poison you right away, but it does irritate the stomach and intestinal lining, and there's no need to take that risk.
- **Washing only the inside, not the outside and bottom**: many people only scrub the inside of a bowl and ignore the outside and the bottom. When bowls are stacked, the bottom of the upper bowl touches the inside of the lower bowl—if the bottom is dirty, you might as well not have washed the lower bowl.
- **Drying before rinsing thoroughly**: putting bowls on the rack with detergent foam still on them. After drying, the detergent forms a dry film on the surface, which enters your body with food the next time it's used. The check is simple: touch it with your finger. If it still feels slippery, it isn't rinsed clean.
- **Leaving dishes overnight**: starch and protein residue left overnight at room temperature dries on very hard, and the next day takes several times the effort to scrub off. More importantly, in summer at room temperature, food residue starts growing large amounts of bacteria within 2–4 hours; an overnight dirty bowl is basically a petri dish. If there are meat scraps, it will also attract cockroaches and fruit flies.

### Tool Use

- **Using steel wool on non-stick pans**: steel wool scratches the non-stick coating (usually Teflon), and once the coating is damaged it keeps peeling. A damaged non-stick pan not only loses its non-stick ability, but flakes of coating also end up in your food. With the coating damaged, the bare pan also sticks and scorches easily. The right way is to clean non-stick pans only with the soft side of a sponge.
- **Washing dishes with a dirty cloth or smelly sponge**: sponges and cloths that stay damp for long periods are themselves breeding grounds for bacteria and mold. "Washing" dishes with a smelly sponge is like wiping bacteria all over them. Same for cloths—drying dishes with a cloth you just used on a greasy stove just moves the dirt around. Squeeze the sponge dry and air it out after each use, and wash cloths at high temperature or replace them regularly.
- **Scraping enamel/ceramic coatings with hard tools**: the surfaces of enamel pots and ceramic-coated cookware are fairly brittle; scraping hard with a metal spatula or steel wool will chip or scratch them. Once the enamel chips and exposes the metal underneath, it not only heats unevenly but also rusts easily.
- **Leaving the tap running full blast the whole time**: running the tap throughout can waste dozens of liters of water per wash. A better way: turn off the water, go over all the dishes with the sponge and dish soap first, then turn on the water and rinse everything together.

### Material Damage

- **Soaking cast iron / carbon steel pans for a long time**: cast iron and carbon steel have no anti-rust coating (or rely on a layer of seasoning to prevent rust), and soaking in water makes them rust quickly. Rust not only looks bad; if it's serious, you'll have to strip and re-season the pan. The right way is to dry it immediately after washing, dry it over low heat, and coat it with a thin layer of cooking oil.
- **Using lots of dish soap on cast iron / carbon steel**: dish soap strips off the seasoning you worked so hard to build, and the pan will stick badly the next time you use it. Day-to-day cleaning of cast iron and carbon steel only needs hot water + a brush; for stubborn residue, scrub with coarse salt.
- **Running cold water into a hot pan**: a pan straight off the stove is very hot, and cold water causes violent thermal expansion and contraction. Stainless steel pans may warp, and cast iron may even crack. If there's still hot oil in the pan, cold water also makes oil and water spatter, which can burn you. Let the pan cool down a little before washing.
- **Soaking wooden cutting boards / spatulas for a long time**: wood swells when it absorbs water and shrinks as it dries; after a few cycles it cracks and warps. Once a wooden cutting board has cracks, food bits and bacteria hide in them and they are very hard to clean. The right way is to wash quickly, dry quickly and stand it upright.

### Hygiene Hazards

- **Putting wet dishes straight into the cupboard**: putting dishes away in a closed cupboard before they're dry gives bacteria and mold a warm, damp, ideal environment. After a few days, mold spots or odors may appear on the dishes, and everything in the cupboard may be affected.
- **Washing raw and cooked items together**: if a cutting board and knife used for raw meat or fish go straight into the dishwater with the other dishes, any salmonella, E. coli and so on from the raw meat spreads through the water to the other tableware. The right way is to wash raw food tools separately at the end, scalding them with hot water or disinfecting them if needed.
- **Never cleaning the drying rack**: if you don't regularly empty and clean the drip tray under the drying rack, the standing water will stink and grow mold, and the bowls on the rack get re-contaminated by the dirty water evaporating from below. Clean the drying rack and drip tray once a week.
- **Water pooling at the bottom of the chopstick holder**: chopsticks stand tips-down in the holder. If the holder has no drainage holes or is never cleaned, water pools at the bottom and gets moldy. The tips are the part that touches food; soaking them in dirty water means eating with moldy chopsticks every meal. Either choose a holder with drainage holes, or place chopsticks tips up.
- **Continuing to use moldy bamboo or wooden chopsticks**: once black mold spots appear on bamboo or wooden chopsticks, the mold has penetrated deep into the wood fibers, and ordinary washing can't remove it. Some molds (such as Aspergillus flavus) produce potent carcinogens. Throw away and replace moldy or cracked bamboo and wooden chopsticks.

### Other Easily Overlooked Pitfalls

- **Loading the dishwasher without scraping**: thinking the dishwasher can wash anything. Large scraps (bones, leaves, rice grains) clog the filter and spray holes, reducing cleaning performance, and in serious cases can even damage the drain pump. At least scrape off obvious solid scraps before loading.
- **Never cleaning the dishwasher**: if the filter, door seal and spray arms inside the dishwasher are never cleaned, grease and mold build up. Not only will the dishes come out smelling, but the dishwasher itself may become a source of contamination. Clean the filter every 1–2 weeks and run an empty cycle with dishwasher cleaner once a month.
- **Washing scraps straight down the drain**: starchy scraps like rice, noodles and vegetable leaves swell and clump in the drain and will clog the pipes over time. Grease poured down the drain solidifies on the pipe walls, speeding up clogs. The right way is to scrape solid scraps into the bin first, and put a strainer over the sink drain to catch anything that slips through.
- **Mixing dish soap with chlorine bleach**: some people add both dish soap and chlorine bleach (such as "84" disinfectant) to the dishwater for "double disinfection", but mixing them may produce harmful gases that irritate the respiratory tract. If you need to disinfect, do it separately after normal washing and rinsing: soak in disinfectant, then rinse with clean water at the end.

## Summary

Cooking turns ingredients into something edible; washing dishes returns the kitchen to a state where you can cook again. Together they form a complete cycle—cook without washing up, and the kitchen soon becomes unusable.

By mastering a scientific way to wash dishes, making good use of modern kitchen tools, and knowing how to handle different materials properly, you can make washing dishes both efficient and safe. Build the habit of "wash right after use, put away right after washing", and cooking becomes something you can keep up for the long term.
