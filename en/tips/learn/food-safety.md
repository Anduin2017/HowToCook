# Food Safety

## Poisoning

The following foods carry a risk of poisoning:

* Unripe green tomatoes
* Undercooked green beans (kidney beans), long beans (cowpeas), [sword beans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canavalia_gladiata)
* Sprouted potatoes (yams), sweet potatoes, peanuts
* Improperly processed/undercooked [daylily flowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemerocallis_citrina) can cause [colchicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colchicine) poisoning
* Raw soy milk
* Wood ear fungus soaked for too long (not just wood ear—any fungus soaked too long carries a poisoning risk)
* Undercooked animal offal
* Mushrooms you don't recognize, or undercooked mushrooms (a Yunnan saying goes: red cap, white stem, eat them and you'll all lie on slabs together. Generally, the prettier the mushroom, the more dangerous it is.)
* …… (contributions welcome)

Storing and cooking acidic foods in aluminum containers for long periods also carries a poisoning risk, for example:

* Pickled cabbage (suancai)
* Dried bamboo shoots
* Ketchup
* Lemon juice
* Braised meat (lurou)
* Soy sauce and pickles
* …… (contributions welcome)

## Allergies

The following are common allergenic foods (note: allergic reactions are generally lifelong):

Adults:

* Shrimp, crab, shellfish (seafood that isn't fresh and has bacterial growth)
* Peanuts
* Tree nuts
* Fish

Children:

* Peanuts
* Tree nuts
* Eggs
* Milk (mainly intolerance to the A1 protein in milk)
* Wheat and soy

## Salmonella Infection

Salmonella is most common in foods of animal origin; vegetables can also carry it if contaminated by feces.

The following foods carry a risk of salmonella infection:

* Undercooked eggs
* Undercooked meat
* Unpasteurized milk

## Aflatoxin

Aflatoxin is commonly produced by Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus parasiticus and a few other molds in moldy grains such as rice, beans and peanuts, and is the most potent carcinogen known so far. It only begins to break down above 280℃, so ordinary cooking doesn't easily destroy it.

The following foods carry a risk of aflatoxin poisoning:

* Spoiled peanuts
* Spoiled rice
* Spoiled corn

Note that this includes products made from them, such as rice noodles and cornmeal; peanut sprouts (sprouted peanuts) grown and produced safely can be considered safe to eat.

## 3-Nitropropionic Acid

3-Nitropropionic acid is produced by the fungus Arthrinium saccharicola, which commonly grows on sugarcane and coconuts. Poisoning mainly damages the central nervous system. Acute symptoms include vomiting, dizziness, paroxysmal convulsions, gaze deviation, coma and even death; after-effects are mainly extrapyramidal damage, with symptoms such as flexion, twisting, spasms, limb rigidity and reduced muscle tone at rest. There is currently no specific antidote for this toxin.

The following foods carry a risk of 3-nitropropionic acid poisoning:

* Red-hearted sugarcane
* [Spoiled coconuts](https://www.bilibili.com/video/BV1w84y147TU)

Besides appearance, smell is also an important criterion. Spoiled plants often give off a distinctive odor (like fermented distillers' grains, sourness, etc.). The best way to handle food that smells spoiled is to throw it away.

## Parasites

Parasites can enter the body through air, drinking water, food and direct contact. If parasites enter the circulatory system, they can attack white blood cells, reach organs such as the lungs and liver, or block blood or lymph vessels, causing diseases such as cirrhosis, portal hypertension and elephantiasis. And if a person becomes an intermediate host of the pork tapeworm, the parasite can even reach the eyes, heart and brain, endangering life.

It's best to make sure the following foods are fully cooked, or they may leave corresponding parasites in your body:

* River snails: Angiostrongylus (rat lungworm)
* Raw fish (sashimi): liver fluke
* Rice field eel: Gnathostoma
* Bullfrog: Spirometra (sparganum)
* Pork: pork tapeworm
* Surface of beef (fine to eat once the surface is cooked): beef tapeworm

## Safe Food Temperatures

Heating food to a high enough temperature and holding it for a certain time reduces the risk of bacteria and parasites surviving to some extent.
Different foods have different temperature requirements; cooks should measure the center temperature of the food with a kitchen thermometer.

To measure temperature, use: a kitchen thermometer
Measure: the center temperature of the food

The following are industry-standard safe food temperatures:

|           | Whole cuts                                            | Ground meat  | Whole bird  |
|-----------|------------------------------------------------|-------|-------|
| Pork      | 71°C                                           | 71°C  |       |
| Poultry      | 74°C                                           | 74°C  | 85°C  |
| Beef/lamb | Medium rare: 63°C; Medium: 71°C; Medium well: 77°C         | 71°C  |       |
| Reheated leftovers | 74°C                                           |       |       |

Based on the latest 2011 standard from the [U.S. Department of Agriculture](https://www.usda.gov/about-usda/news/blog/cooking-meat-check-new-recommended-temperatures), safe cooking temperatures have been updated as follows:

|           | Whole cuts                                            | Ground meat  |
|-----------|------------------------------------------------|-------|
| Pork      | 63°C (rest 3 minutes after removing from heat)                 | 71°C  |
| Poultry      | 74°C                                           | 74°C  |
| Beef/lamb | 63°C (rest 3 minutes after removing from heat)                 | 71°C  |
