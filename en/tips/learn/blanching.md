# Blanching

Blanching is a step in cooking, called chaoshui (焯水) in Chinese, pronounced chāo shuǐ.

Blanching means heating prepped ingredients in a pot of boiling water until half or fully cooked, then taking them out for further cooking or seasoning.

Blanching is an indispensable step in cooking, especially for cold dishes. It plays a key role in a dish's color, aroma and flavor—especially color.

Most vegetables, and meats with a fishy or gamey smell, need blanching.

## Methods

### Boiling-Water Blanching

Boiling-water blanching means heating the water in the pot first, then adding the ingredients. Turn them promptly once they're in, keep it short, and don't overcook.

This method is mostly used for plant ingredients such as celery, spinach and celtuce. Pay special attention to the heat: if it goes even slightly too long, the color fades and the vegetables lose their crispness and tenderness. So once they're in the pot, take them out to cool as soon as the water starts simmering again.

- Leafy vegetables should be blanched before slicing, to avoid losing too many nutrients.
- Use plenty of water and high heat so the water returns to a boil quickly after adding the ingredients; for green leafy vegetables, take them out after a brief boil.
- Cool and drain vegetables immediately after blanching, so residual heat doesn't turn them yellow or mushy.
- When blanching vegetables, you can add a little vegetable oil such as peanut, corn or soybean oil to keep them bright green.

### Cold-Water Blanching

Cold-water blanching means putting the ingredients and cold water into the pot together. The water should cover the ingredients; then bring it to a boil. The goal is to cook the ingredients for further preparation.

Potatoes, carrots and the like are bulky and slow to cook, so they need to boil longer.

Some animal ingredients, such as plain pork, beef omasum and beef tripe, are also put in cold water and heated until cooked before further preparation. Some animal ingredients used for soup should also go in cold water, so nutrients gradually seep out as they heat and the soup is flavorful; using hot water would make the proteins coagulate.

- Don't add too much water to the pot—just enough to cover the ingredients.
- As it heats gradually, turn the ingredients frequently so they heat evenly and are properly blanched.

## Additional Notes

- Blanching sometimes causes unstable, soluble nutrients to leach out of ingredients; water-soluble vitamins in fresh vegetables are especially prone to loss
- Blanch animal and plant ingredients separately; blanch strongly and mildly colored/flavored ingredients separately; blanch large and small pieces separately, to prevent flavors mixing
- After blanching animal ingredients, the liquid can be skimmed, clarified and used as stock

### Blanching Meat

- After blanching in boiling water, meat is ready once it changes color; take it out, drain, and go on to the next step.
- After blanching meat, rinse off any clinging blood scum—remember to use warm water, otherwise the meat contracts as it cools and traps the dirt, so you can't wash the scum off.

### Blanching Leafy Greens

- When washing leafy greens, sprinkle some salt into the water; this helps wash out any bugs
- Plunge blanched greens into cold water immediately to keep their color and texture. If you don't, the residual heat makes them lose their crunch and turn mushy
