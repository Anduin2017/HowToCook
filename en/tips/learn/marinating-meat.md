# Marinating (Meat)

## Note

Marinating as described here is a step in preparing ingredients before cooking, not making finished products such as cured pork or sausages

## Marinating

Marinating meat before cooking is a common way to season it in advance. Usually raw meat is marinated. Decide how to cut the meat based on the dish.

  For example, for popcorn chicken, chicken breast is cut into dice-sized pieces and then marinated in a bowl
  
  For example, for whole roast lamb, a leg of lamb, or a half or whole side of lamb, you don't need to cut it—just rub plenty of seasoning over the surface to marinate it

Depending on the dish, the seasonings and supporting ingredients for the marinade can be anything. Sometimes, for different flavors, supporting ingredients may also need to be prepared in advance.

## Basic Concepts of Marinating

This describes marinating for a normal level of seasoning.

- In general, the more meat (e.g. marinating 5kg of chicken wings at once), the bigger the piece (e.g. a whole leg of lamb), and the stronger the flavor you want, the more seasonings and supporting ingredients you need
- In general, the longer you plan to marinate, the less seasoning and supporting ingredients you should use
- The marinade should coat all surfaces evenly. For meat slices or shreds, mix and massage them by hand as evenly as possible. For a whole leg of lamb, brush the marinade evenly over the surface with your hand or a brush
- Meat for stir-frying or deep-frying usually needs marinating in advance. Stir-fried meat should stay tender, and cooking usually uses high heat for a short time. When short cooking makes it hard for flavor to penetrate, marinating in advance makes up for it

## Marinating Techniques

- Fine meat shreds, thin slices: the meat is delicate, so be as gentle as possible. Shape your fingers like the claw of a claw machine and lightly mix in the marinade. Then gently stir in one direction
- Meat shreds, slices, chunks: same technique as above, but you can use a bit more force
- Chicken drumsticks, wings and similar sizes: first score the ingredient a few times. Chicken drumettes and drumsticks can be pierced all the way through with a knife. Then mix the marinade in a bowl first, and put the ingredients into the bowl to coat evenly
- Leg of lamb and similar sizes: usually pierce holes all the way through the thicker parts. Then mix the marinade and spread it evenly over the surface

## Marinating Containers and Time

- Any container that holds the ingredients and marinade will do, including bowls, plates and trays. This is open marinating, usually for a shorter time, commonly 0.5-2 hours. (Roast) leg of lamb can be marinated this way too, but for longer
- You can marinate in a large enough food-grade zip-top bag. This is sealed marinating, usually for a long time, e.g. overnight, or for things like pork ribs that are slow to absorb flavor. Commonly 4 hours to overnight. Use slightly less marinade so the dish doesn't end up too strong and salty

## Common Marinade Ingredients

- Light soy sauce: adds a savory soy base with saltiness. Can be used with almost any meat
- Dark soy sauce: not very salty, but colors easily. Used for base color and aroma. Generally don't use a lot, to avoid a beany taste. Can be used with almost any red meat (used sparingly); for pork liver and the like you can add more
- Salt: salty, but without a soy aroma after cooking. Can be used with any meat
- White (granulated) sugar: adds sweetness (in large amounts), or makes meat more tender (in small amounts). Can be used with any poultry or livestock meat, but not commonly with fish and seafood.
- Brown sugar: adds sweetness and its distinctive brown sugar taste, slightly stronger than white sugar. Can be used with almost any meat (usually darker meats or dishes with a darker finished color)
- Oyster sauce: adds savory umami, saltiness and sweetness. Usually used with red meat
- White vinegar/rice vinegar: adds sourness. Used less often
- Aged vinegar/black vinegar: adds not only sourness but also aroma and color. Black vinegar suits darker fish (especially grilled fish)
- Cooking wine: removes off-odors and adds aroma. Can be used with almost any meat. But note:
  - Cooking wine has a strong flavor of its own that easily masks the ingredient's natural aroma; for ingredients without a strong fishy smell, consider not using cooking wine to remove odors. For example: beef, fish and chicken.
  - For chicken, you can use baijiu instead of cooking wine.
  - For beef, you can use green onion and ginger water instead of cooking wine.
  - For white fish, just washing off the blood and slime removes most of the fishy smell; no cooking wine is recommended.
- Huangjiu (Shaoxing wine): removes off-odors and adds aroma, more effective than cooking wine and with a more complex aroma. Usually used with white meat. It can also be used with red meat, but the effect is about the same as cooking wine.
- Five-spice powder/thirteen-spice powder: adds aroma to meat; the simplest compound spices. Five-spice only adds aroma, while thirteen-spice has a distinctive, recognizable aroma. Control the amount when marinating with these spices. Can be used with almost any meat, but not commonly with fish and seafood
- Chili powder: there are many kinds. Setting aside the chili variety, by grind there's chili powder/chili flakes, crushed chili, etc. Besides adding heat to meat, it also adds color to the dish. Avoid using too much chili when marinating. Can be used with almost any meat that needs a spicy base, but control the cooking time so the chili doesn't turn bitter or too dark
- Ground cumin, ground fennel: usually use the ground powder, not whole seeds, so the meat absorbs the flavor more easily. Can be used with almost any red meat and chicken
- X-pepper powders: add pungent, spicy, aromatic and sharp flavors to meat. Use in moderation so they don't overpower other flavors
  - Ground black pepper: pungent and spicy. Can be used with almost any red meat
  - Ground white pepper: pungent and aromatic. A bit milder than black pepper, emphasizing aroma. Can be used with almost any meat
  - Ground Sichuan pepper: pungent and sharp. Has the distinctive aroma of Sichuan peppercorns and is quite recognizable. Can be used with almost any meat
- Doubanjiang: adds fermented bean paste aroma, saltiness and heat to meat. Can be used with almost any red meat
- Green onion, ginger, garlic: green onion and ginger remove off-odors and add aroma; garlic adds a pungent aroma. Green onion can be cut into sections or slices as needed; ginger is usually sliced, sometimes peeled; garlic can be sliced or minced. If you don't want green onion and ginger in the finished dish, or want a lighter flavor, squeeze pieces of them in a bowl with a tiny bit of water to make green onion and ginger water, and marinate the meat in that. Garlic is usually not added directly. Can be used with any meat
- Hoisin sauce, shrimp paste, etc.: add umami and saltiness to meat. Hoisin is on the sweet side, shrimp paste is strong. Can be used with almost any meat, but not in many situations
- Douchi (fermented black beans): adds fermented bean aroma and saltiness to meat. Can be used with almost any red meat, but not used much
- Starch (shengfen): i.e. starch. Starch is an important marinade ingredient for velveting. The thicker the coating, or the silkier the texture you want, the more starch you need. Can be used with almost any meat. `Starch can be used as a simple coating for deep-frying (usually with flour or similar added as needed); in that case it's generally not added during marinating`
  - Cornstarch, potato starch: usually the stickiest
  - Sweet potato starch: slightly less sticky
- Oil: adding a suitable amount of oil while marinating seals in moisture and flavor. If marinating in an open container for a long time (e.g. in a bowl), an oil seal greatly helps keep the meat from drying out or turning tough. Can be used with almost any meat. After an oil seal, use slightly less oil in the wok when stir-frying; no difference for deep-frying

## Some General-Purpose Marinade Formulas

- Beef: marinate with a moderate amount of light soy sauce, a little cooking wine and a little sugar. Depending on taste, add salt (for extra saltiness), oyster sauce and a tiny bit of hoisin (oyster sauce beef), five-spice/thirteen-spice (onion beef stir-fry). Use green onion and ginger sparingly
- Chicken (including breast and wings): marinate with a moderate amount of light soy sauce, a smaller amount of sugar and a little cooking wine. Depending on taste, add salt (for extra saltiness), five-spice/thirteen-spice (popcorn chicken), a tiny bit of dark soy sauce (pan-fried chicken wings)
- White fish: marinate with a moderate amount of salt and a little cooking wine/huangjiu. Depending on taste, add hoisin/seafood soy sauce/steamed fish soy sauce (pan-fried hairtail), green onion and ginger (water) (roast skin-on fish)
- Red fish: marinate with a moderate amount of light soy sauce and a little cooking wine. Depending on taste, add seafood soy sauce/a little steamed fish soy sauce (pan-fried salmon), brown sugar (Nordic-style roast salmon)
- Pork liver: marinate with a moderate amount of light soy sauce and cooking wine. Depending on taste, add starch and a moderate amount of dark soy sauce (velvet-fried pork liver), a little sugar, etc.

## Practical Dish Examples

- Onion beef stir-fry: for 150g of beef (one serving). Slice the beef; the finished dish should be tender and smooth; stir-fried
  - Light soy sauce 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - White sugar 2.5-10g (about 1-4 teaspoons, depending on how sweet you like it)
  - Ground cumin 5g (about 2 teaspoons)
  - Starch 10-15g (about 1 small handful)
  - Oil 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - (Optional) Thirteen-spice 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground black pepper 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)

- Oyster sauce beef: for 150g of beef (one serving). Slice the beef; the finished dish should be tender with a good velvety coating, and on the sweet side; stir-fried
  - Light soy sauce 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - Cooking wine 5ml (about 1 tablespoon)
  - Oyster sauce 10-20ml (about 2-4 tablespoons, depending on how salty you like it; oyster sauce is fairly salty)
  - White sugar 5-15g (about 2-6 teaspoons, depending on how sweet you like it)
  - Starch 25-35g (about 1 large handful)
  - Oil 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)

- Five-spice salt and pepper chicken: for 150g of chicken breast (one serving). Cut the chicken into dice-shaped pieces; deep-fried
  - Light soy sauce 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - Five-spice powder 5g (about 2 teaspoons) or thirteen-spice 2.5-5g (about 1-2 teaspoons)
  - (Optional) Ground cumin 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground white pepper 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)

- Honey-glazed roast chicken wings: for 250g of bone-in chicken wing flats (one serving). Score the wings a few times; the finished dish is savory-sweet with an emphasis on sweet; roasted
  - Light soy sauce 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - White sugar 5-15g (about 2-6 teaspoons, depending on how sweet you like it)
  - Honey/syrup 10-20ml (about 2-4 tablespoons, depending on how sweet you like it. If using 10g or more of white sugar, add only 10ml)
  - (Optional) Five-spice powder 2.5g (about 1 teaspoon. Don't use thirteen-spice)

- Roast salmon: for a 200g boneless salmon fillet (one serving). Don't cut the fish; roasted in the oven
  - Light soy sauce 10ml (about 2 tablespoons)
  - Cooking wine 2.5ml (about 0.5 tablespoon)
  - Brown sugar 10-20g (about 4-8 teaspoons, depending on how sweet you like it)
  - Balsamic vinegar/Zhenjiang black vinegar 2.5-5ml (about 0.5-1 tablespoon, depending on how sour you like it)
  - Ground nutmeg 2.5g (about 1 teaspoon)
  - Ground thyme 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - Ground ginger 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - Ground rosemary 1-2g (about 0.5-1 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Ground white pepper 1g (about 0.5 teaspoon)
  - (Optional) Dried chili flakes 2.5-10g (about 1-4 teaspoons, depending on how spicy you like it)
