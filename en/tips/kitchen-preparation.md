## Choosing Cooking Oil

Before choosing an oil, you need some basic knowledge about fatty acids.

### Types of Fatty Acids

Fatty acids are divided into:

* Saturated fatty acids (avoid when possible)
* Unsaturated fatty acids
  * Cis fatty acids
  * Trans fatty acids (especially avoid)
  * Polyunsaturated fatty acids
  * Monounsaturated fatty acids

Saturated fatty acids are solid at room temperature, while unsaturated fatty acids are liquid at room temperature.

### Fatty Acids to Avoid

Among them, **saturated fatty acids** and **trans fatty acids** are generally considered unhealthy.

Saturated fatty acids increase the risk of obesity, high cholesterol and heart disease.

Research shows that long-term excessive consumption of trans fatty acids produced by hydrogenation can cause abnormal blood lipid metabolism, increasing the risk of cardiovascular disease. Some studies also show it may increase the risk of chronic diseases such as diabetes and obesity.

The World Health Organization recommends that, to improve cardiovascular health, trans fatty acids in the diet should be kept as low as possible, with a maximum intake of no more than 1% of total energy. In other words, if an adult consumes an average of 2000 kcal per day, trans fat intake should not exceed 2.2 grams per day.

The Chinese national standard GB 28050-2011 requires that when food ingredients contain, or the production process uses, hydrogenated and/or partially hydrogenated oils, the trans fat (fatty acid) content must also be shown on the nutrition facts label.

### Trans Fatty Acids in Food

According to relevant surveys, the average trans fatty acid content of baked goods (pastries, biscuits, bread, etc.), condiments and fried foods is between 0.30 and 0.50 g/100g.

So there is no need to worry too much: the trans fatty acids in everyday food are not enough to harm your health. But just in case, when buying snacks, it doesn't hurt to check the `trans fat` content on the nutrition facts label.

### Trans Fatty Acids in Cooking

A 2021 survey showed that the average trans fatty acid content of vegetable oils in China is 0.86 g/100g, so there is no need to worry too much.

The source of trans fatty acids that needs extra attention is the cooking process:

Vegetable oils often contain a high proportion of polyunsaturated fatty acids, which have poor thermal stability and easily turn into trans fats at high temperatures.

So we need to choose oils sensibly for different situations, and keep the time the oil is heated as short as possible.

### Choosing a Vegetable Oil

| Oil | Saturated fat (%) | Omega 3 (%) | Omega 6 (%) | Omega 9 (%) |
| :----: | :----: | :----: | :----: | :----: |
| Canola oil | 7% | 11% | 21% | 61% |
| Flaxseed oil | 9% | 57% | 16% | 18% |
| Sunflower oil | 12% | 1% | 71% | 16% |
| Corn oil | 13% | 1% | 57% | 29% |
| Olive oil | 15% | 1% | 9% | 75% |
| Soybean oil | 15% | 8% | 54% | 23% |
| Peanut oil | 19% | 0% | 33% | 48% |
| Cottonseed oil | 27% | 0% | 54% | 19% |
| Lard | 43% | 1% | 9% | 47% |
| Palm oil | 51% | 0% | 10% | 39% |
| Beef tallow | 68% | 1% | 3% | 28% |
| Coconut oil | 91% | 0% | 2% | 7% |

* `Peanut oil` is rich in `monounsaturated fat`. Only choose high-quality peanut oil, though, and avoid heating it too long during cooking so it doesn't produce `trans fatty acids`.
* `Olive oil` is rich in `monounsaturated fat`, which has only one unsaturated bond. Olive oil is low in `saturated fatty acids`.
    * **Cold dishes / low-temperature cooking:** choose **extra virgin olive oil**. It keeps more antioxidants such as polyphenols, but its smoke point is relatively low (about 160-190℃), so it is not suitable for high-heat stir-frying.
    * **Medium to high heat stir-frying:** choose **refined olive oil** (labeled Pure or Refined). Its smoke point is higher (above 230℃), making it better suited to Chinese cooking.
* `Soybean oil` contains about 15% `saturated fatty acids` and also contains linoleic acid and vitamins. But soybean oil is high in polyunsaturated fatty acids, which are unstable and easily produce `trans fatty acids` during processing, so it is not recommended for long-term high-heat stir-frying. It can be used for cold dishes or ordinary stewing.
* `Rapeseed oil` has good thermal stability.
    * **Traditional rapeseed oil:** may contain high levels of erucic acid; consuming large amounts over a long period may be bad for cardiovascular health.
    * **Double-low rapeseed oil (i.e. Canola Oil):** has been improved to have very low erucic acid (<2%) and an excellent fatty acid profile (high in monounsaturated fat, and contains Omega-3). It is currently a cooking oil highly recommended by nutritionists for everyday stir-frying.
* `Coconut oil` is very high in `saturated fatty acids` and has excellent thermal stability, but note that some foods use hydrogenated coconut oil. It is suitable for frying in the kitchen.
* `Palm oil` is very high in `saturated fatty acids` and has good thermal stability. It is often used in commercial frying, and eating it often increases the risk of high cholesterol.
* Animal fats such as `lard` and `beef tallow` are rich in `saturated fatty acids` and have excellent thermal stability. Although they don't easily produce harmful substances at high temperatures, their potential negative impact on cardiovascular health means you should limit your intake. They are not recommended as your only long-term cooking oil.

So, based on the table above, we can draw some conclusions:

* No oil is perfect; each has pros and cons. Choose different oils for different cooking situations (cold, hot, frying).
* **Avoid using a single oil:** modern people often consume too much Omega-6 and far too little Omega-3. Keep 2-3 different types of oil at home and alternate between them.
* To avoid consuming too many `trans fatty acids`, don't use heat-unstable oils (such as soybean oil or corn oil for high-heat stir-frying) when heating, and don't heat oil for too long.
* Don't eat large amounts of fried food. Although saturated fat is thermally stable, eating too much saturated fat is itself a health burden.
* Don't reuse oil. Repeatedly heated oil produces large amounts of `trans fatty acids` and oxidation products.
* Don't eat takeout food for long periods, because it is hard to know what oil they used (usually cheap, unstable oil).

#### Stir-frying Oil (Medium Heat)

* Canola oil (double-low rapeseed oil)
* Peanut oil
* Refined olive oil

These oils contain more monounsaturated fatty acids and have moderate or high smoke points, so they can handle most Chinese stir-frying needs.

#### Frying Oil (High Heat)

* Coconut oil
* Palm oil
* Lard / beef tallow

High-heat stir-frying and deep-frying need more heat-stable saturated fats, which produce fewer harmful oxidation products. But for cardiovascular health, **the most fundamental strategy is to fry less often**.

#### Oil for Cold Dishes, Low-Temperature Cooking and Stewing

* Flaxseed oil
* Perilla oil
* Extra virgin olive oil
* Sesame oil / walnut oil

Flaxseed oil and perilla oil are rich in extremely valuable **Omega-3**, but they are extremely sensitive to heat; even a little heating makes them oxidize, spoil and develop off-flavors. Using them for cold dishes is the "perfect strategy" for getting more Omega-3.
