# How to Make Chiffon Cake

This beginner baking dessert is delicate and soft with a cloud-like, airy texture, a classic basic Western pastry. The main ingredients, eggs and milk, provide high-quality protein and calcium. The process is demanding when it comes to whipping, folding and baking temperature, so it's somewhat challenging, but with patience and by following the steps, beginners can succeed. It generally takes 1.5 to 2 hours from prep to out of the oven.

Estimated difficulty: ★★★★★

Estimated calories: 274 kcal

## Ingredients and Tools

### Tools

* Oven (a rice cooker can substitute, but it usually fails because of the inner pot material)
* Whisk (electric is best; manual is tiring and has some chance of failure) or chopsticks (strongly not recommended)
* Anodized aluminum pan (never use a non-stick pan; common sizes are 6-inch or 8-inch)
* Spatula (for folding the batter)

### Ingredients

- Eggs
- White sugar
- Milk (or water)
- Cooking oil (or butter, softened by warming)
- Cake flour (Huiyi brand recommended)
- [Optional] Lemon juice or white vinegar

## Calculation

Each portion is based on 1 egg (normal medium size, about 50g).

| Ingredient | 1 portion (base) | 6-inch (3 portions, area 36) | 8-inch (5 portions, area 64) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Eggs** | 1 | 3 | 5 |
| **White sugar** | 16g | 50g | 80g |
| **Cooking oil** | 8g | 25g | 40g |
| **Milk** | 10g | 30g | 50g |
| **Cake flour** | 17g | 50g | 90g |

## Steps

### Separating the Eggs

1. Take fresh eggs out of the refrigerator
2. Prepare two containers and wipe them dry, one for the whites and one for the yolks
3. The container for the whites may have a few drops of water, but **must not have any oil at all**; the container for the yolks must not have water droplets
4. Crack the eggs and separate the whites and yolks into the two containers, by hand or with an egg separator.
5. Don't break the yolks while separating. **The whites must not contain any yolk at all**, or whipping will be seriously affected. (The white chalazae can go in with the whites; it doesn't matter)
6. (Note: without a stand mixer, the container for the whites is also the whipping bowl. To avoid overflow, once all the whites are in, they shouldn't fill more than **1/8** of the container)

### Mixing the Yolk Batter (Yolks-Last Method)

1. Prepare a new empty bowl and add **all of the cooking oil** and **all of the milk** from the recipe
2. Whisk quickly by hand until the oil and milk are fully combined into a milky white suspension (emulsified)
3. Sift **all of the cake flour** from the recipe into the emulsion. Mix with the spatula in a "Z pattern" until there's no obvious dry flour (the batter may look a bit rough at this point; that's normal)
4. Add **all of the separated yolks** to the bowl and keep mixing with the spatula in a "Z pattern"
5. The batter will now become very smooth and silky, and won't easily develop gluten. Set it aside

### Whipping the Whites

1. Take the remaining **3/4** of the sugar and divide it into three portions, each **1/4** of the total
2. Add lemon juice or white vinegar to the whites (optional)
3. With the mixer on medium, whip the whites until they have *large, coarse bubbles*, then add the **first portion of sugar**
4. With the mixer on high, whip until the *bubbles are finer*, then add the **second portion of sugar**
5. With the mixer on high, whip to "soft peaks", then add the **third portion of sugar**

> Visual cue: when you lift the whisk, the meringue forms a **long peak that curves downward** (commonly called a "big hook"). This stage is suitable for cake rolls.

6. With the mixer on medium-low, whip to "stiff peaks"

> Visual cue: when you lift the whisk, the meringue forms a **short, upright, firm peak** (commonly called a "little straight point"); if you turn the bowl upside down, the meringue sticks firmly to the bottom and doesn't slide at all. This is the standard stage needed for chiffon cake.

7. The whites are now whipped as required

  * For judging the meringue stages, see the pictures in the linked reference.)
  * Keep the whisk close to the bottom of the bowl, so you don't end up with whipped foam floating on top and liquid still at the bottom)

### Combining

1. Give the yolk batter a quick stir
2. With the spatula, take **1/3** of the meringue and add it to the yolk batter
3. Use a "folding" motion, to avoid knocking out the air

  * The folding motion is:
  * Holding the spatula in your right hand, insert it into the center of the bowl down to the bottom of the batter
  * Scrape toward 8 o'clock until you hit the side of the bowl, scoop up the batter and lift it into the air, then move back to the center and drop the batter into the bowl
  * With your left hand, turn the bowl from 9 o'clock to 7 o'clock, exactly 60 degrees, completing one cycle
  * The speed is about two times per second
  * This method comes from *Kojima Rumi's Cake Classroom*. In plain words: toss it like you're stir-frying.

4. Pour the mixture of **1/3** meringue and yolk batter into the remaining **2/3** of the meringue, and keep folding until even
5. Pour the batter into the pan and tap it a few times to get rid of large bubbles

### Baking

1. Total baking time: **30-35** minutes for a 6-inch cake, **50** minutes for an 8-inch cake. Adjust flexibly to your oven, generally within $\pm 5$ minutes. (Watch through the oven door during the last few minutes)
2. Preheat with **top element at **150** degrees Celsius and bottom element at **160** degrees Celsius**; it takes about 10 minutes to reach temperature.
3. Once preheated, put the pan on the lower rack of the oven
4. Use **two-stage baking**.

  * Stage one oven settings: top element **150** degrees Celsius, bottom element **160** degrees Celsius;
  * The first **3/5** of the total baking time is stage one
  * Stage two settings: top element **160** degrees Celsius, bottom element **170** degrees Celsius;
  * The last **2/5** of the total baking time is stage two. Just change the oven temperature directly to switch.

5. When done, take it out of the oven

  * This may **burn your hands**; use a towel

### Special Instructions for an Air Fryer (6-inch Example)

An air fryer has very strong hot air circulation and the heating element is very close, so using oven temperatures directly will burn the top while leaving the inside raw. Strictly follow this lower-temperature and covering procedure:

1. Preheat: set the air fryer to **130°C** and preheat for **5 minutes**
2. Load and cover: put the pan of batter into the air fryer. **Immediately cover the top of the pan with a sheet of aluminum foil** (shiny side up, matte side facing the food), crimping the edges tightly so the hot air doesn't blow it off
3. Stage one: set **130°C** and bake for **35 minutes**
4. Stage two, browning: open the air fryer and **remove the foil**. Raise the temperature to **150°C** and bake for another **10–15 minutes**
5. Checking doneness: it's ready when the top is puffed and golden. Insert a toothpick into the center; if it comes out without wet batter, it's done
6. Tapping out the hot air and cooling upside down after baking is exactly the same as the oven version

### Cooling and Unmolding

1. (Optional) Drop the pan from a height to knock out the hot air inside
2. Turn the pan upside down for 10 minutes to let the cake cool

  - Unmolding before the cake has cooled will damage it
  - This may **burn your hands**; use a towel

3. Unmold and eat

## Additional Notes

- The following tutorial was used as a reference. It explains things in great detail with a photo for every step. It also explains why each step is done, the principles behind it, and analyzes certain problems:
- [I used a whole crate of eggs to perfect this chiffon cake, and said goodbye to sunken bottoms and collapses](https://zhuanlan.zhihu.com/p/86865919)
- For chiffon cake, whipping the whites is a secondary issue; the key is **the baking temperature and time**.
- The container for the whites may have water droplets; the container for the yolks may not.
  - Reason: oil affects whipping the whites. Egg white is 85% water, so a few drops of water don't affect whipping.
  - Very fresh eggs have stiffer whites. To handle stiff whites, adding 15ml of water for the 5-egg recipe helps the whites whip (3g of water for the 1-egg recipe)
- The sugar added while whipping actually dissolves first in the water of the whites, forming a syrup solution that surrounds the bubbles and protects them.
- Temperature greatly affects how fast and how much sugar dissolves in water, and whites straight out of the refrigerator are hard to whip. But cold eggs are easier to separate, so it's recommended to let them return to room temperature after separating before whipping.
