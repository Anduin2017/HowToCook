# How to Make Twice-Cooked Pork

Twice-cooked pork (huiguo rou) is a famous traditional Sichuan dish made with "second-cut" pork (from the rump) or pork belly. The finished dish is glossy red: the fat turns translucent and slightly curled, the lean is dry-fragrant without being tough, rich but not greasy, savory and slightly spicy, with the combined aroma of garlic sprouts and doubanjiang. Pork is rich in high-quality protein and essential fatty acids, and green and red chilies and garlic sprouts add vitamins. There are a few more steps—boiling, slicing and stir-frying—and some demands on heat control and knife skills, but beginners can succeed by following the process. It takes about 40 minutes in total.

Estimated difficulty: ★★★★

Estimated calories: 997 kcal

## Ingredients and Tools

- Pork belly
- Green onion
- Ginger
- Green and red chili peppers
- Garlic sprouts
- Cooking wine
- Doubanjiang (chili bean paste)
- Light soy sauce
- MSG

## Calculation

- Pork belly: 250g per person with a big appetite, 150g for a smaller appetite (authentic twice-cooked pork uses "second-cut" pork [commonly called rump point], which is firm with the right balance of fat and lean)
- Green onion 2 stalks
- Ginger 10-40g
- Green and red chili peppers (0-30g, depending on your spice tolerance) *Note: thick-fleshed bell peppers are not recommended
- Garlic sprouts 1 bunch
- Cooking wine 5ml
- Doubanjiang 10ml
- MSG 5g
- Light soy sauce 5ml

## Steps

### Pork Belly, Stage One

1. Heat the wok and press the pork belly firmly against it by hand to sear the skin

  - This step deals with the sweat glands in the pork skin (or ask the butcher to scorch the skin when you buy it; if you like the sweat glands, ignore this)

2. Scrub the skin clean with steel wool until all the black, charred parts are completely removed. If not scrubbed clean, it will taste bitter
3. Put the pork belly in the pot, add cold water to cover, and add ginger slices, cooking wine and green onions (tie 2 green onions in a knot)
4. Boil over high heat. Once boiling, skim off the foam and keep boiling for 15 minutes, until a chopstick easily pierces the lean part

### Preparing the Vegetables

1. Cut the green and red chilies into rings
2. Cut the garlic sprouts into segments
3. Cut the ginger into small, thin slices
4. Mix 5ml of doubanjiang and 5ml of light soy sauce in advance

### Pork Belly, Stage Two

1. Take out the boiled pork belly and cool it in cold water
2. Pat the pork belly dry and cut into 2mm thin slices with fat on top and lean on the bottom (thicker slices have a worse texture and are very greasy)

### Stir-frying the Pork

Note: **work quickly in this stage and be careful not to burn anything**

1. Heat the wok and coat it with a layer of oil
2. Add the pork belly and stir-fry until the fat is translucent and the slices curl slightly (known as "lamp cups"); second-cut pork works best.
3. Pour in the doubanjiang and soy sauce mixture and 5g of MSG and stir-fry for 15 seconds
4. Add the chili rings and ginger slices, plus another 5ml of doubanjiang, and stir-fry for 30 seconds
5. Add the garlic sprouts and stir-fry for 60 seconds
6. Serve

### Simplified Version

1. Leave frozen pork belly at room temperature for 0.5 hours, or put fresh pork belly in the refrigerator for 1 hour, then cut into 2-5 mm thin slices
2. Over medium heat, dry-fry the chilies in the wok for 30-45 seconds, then remove
3. Heat the wok, coat it with a layer of oil, add the ginger slices and stir-fry for 15 seconds
4. Add the pork belly, tossing every 10 seconds, until it turns golden brown (the longer you fry it, the firmer the pork)
5. Add the dry-fried chilies and 10ml of doubanjiang, season with light soy sauce, and keep stir-frying for 60 seconds
6. Add the garlic sprout segments and stir-fry for 10 seconds
7. Serve

## Additional Notes

- If you don't like garlic sprouts, use onion or other vegetables instead, but pay attention to how quickly each vegetable cooks and stir-fry it in advance if needed, or it may end up half-cooked
- If the pork is a large piece, cut it into 5 cm square blocks, and boil until a chopstick can pierce the skin with a little force
- Cooling the boiled pork in cold water makes the meat firmer
- Patting the pork dry prevents oil from spattering and burning you when stir-frying
- Remember not to cut the slices too thick, or it will be very greasy
